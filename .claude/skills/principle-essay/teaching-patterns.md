# teaching-patterns

Reference dictionary for the `principle-essay` skill. Every entry is attributed
to the teacher it was measured or transcribed from. Nothing here is invented;
if a pattern has no quote under it, it does not belong in this file.

Sources: **Jared Halverson** (Unshaken), **Todd McLaughlin** (Stick of Joseph),
**Andrea Woodmansee** (Stick of Joseph). Corpus sizes differ; Woodmansee's
counts are over 26,800 words.

Contents:

1. The measured baseline
2. The definition pattern — five moves
3. Scaffold shapes
4. Transition vocabulary
5. The tic blocklist, with substitutes
6. Spoken-only devices and their prose replacements
7. Verbatim model passages
8. Where the three are incompatible

## 2. The definition pattern — five moves

A doctrinal essay's first job is to define a term, and all three converge on
the same five-move shape.

### Move 1 — State the received meaning in its strongest form and grant it

Not a strawman. The version a thoughtful reader actually holds, given a full
clause of respect.

> "Two-way promise, right? Which isn't a wrong answer, but it's not a
> sufficient, it's not a comprehensive answer." — **McLaughlin**

> "certainly all of that is true, but I think there's a big piece that we're
> missing." — **Woodmansee**

> "Certainly that happens in some cases. But I don't believe that was the
> original meaning." — **Woodmansee**

> "Now we talk about being a peculiar people, and we think of peculiar as in
> weird. And yes, sometimes we live up to that." — **Halverson**

Halverson goes furthest and concedes the underlying *value* of the objection
outright — on inclusivity, "and that's a good thing" — before criticizing the
overcorrection. The concession is not tactical politeness. It is what buys the
reading that follows.

### Move 2 — Withhold the term while you build what it needs

Three routes. Pick by term type.

**Prerequisite chain** — McLaughlin, for system terms. Dominion, then the
unseen world, then agency, then veils, and only then altar. Nothing is named
until everything it rests on is standing.

**Withheld cultural fact** — Woodmansee, for ritual and narrative terms. The
token at the door, the wooden bolt, the recognition of the voice. The fact is
the *release* of a tension she built, never the opening.

**Etymology decomposed** — Halverson, for names and loaded words.

> "Beth, beit means house and lechem means bread." — **Halverson**

### Move 3 — The definition: one short present-tense sentence, verb *is* or *means*

Never "X is defined as."

> "an order is a pattern." — **McLaughlin**

> "sin is resistance to the spirit or the light of God in any domain."
> — **McLaughlin**

> "an altar is how you traverse a veil. It's how you part one."
> — **McLaughlin**

> "righteousness just means that you are rightly aligned to a domain of God."
> — **McLaughlin**

> "veiling was considered to be a mark of sanctity." — **Woodmansee** (eight
> words, after the ground is cleared)

> "Endowment means gift." — the tradition; three words, and the whole
> definition

**Sub-rules.** If the term is abstract, choose a deliberately **generic genus
and load the differentia** — McLaughlin's "a structure," "a practice," "a
system or a platform." If the term is a role or an ordinance, **define by
function**: an altar is *how you traverse a veil*. Halverson's shape is the
paired negation, used once: "X doesn't mean A. It means B." McLaughlin's is the
deflationary *just*, which tells the reader the definition is smaller than they
were bracing for.

### Move 4 — Cash it out in the same breath

No definition is allowed to stand as information.

> "So Bethlehem means house of bread. What better place for the the bread of
> life himself to be born?" — **Halverson**

> "The Hebrew words for eternity and conceal actually share the same root...
> So eternity, in a sense, is something that is concealed." — **Halverson**

### Move 5 — Test it on the inverse or the edge

> "Satan or the adversary or the dark domains do not have altars that are
> independent of themselves." — **McLaughlin**, running the altar definition
> against Cain immediately

> "But the interesting thing is, it's not a wall right it's a veil and so a
> veil is meant to be traversed." — **Woodmansee**, correcting her own
> inversion before it overshoots

Halverson's version of Move 5 is to pair the term with its twin in a column:
justification and sanctification, works and grace, replace and succeed.

### The three governing constraints

**The hedge budget is one.** McLaughlin's frame-hedge is deliberate and
doctrinally motivated — he offers definitions as instruments rather than
verdicts ("I hate saying this is the definition of this, right? This is maybe a
really good way of thinking about it"). Woodmansee's stack is the failure mode:
"So it's possible that... I find it to be very likely that... I think... I
believe..." — three hedges in four sentences, which on a page reads as evasion.

**The definition is the reward, not the setup.** When the payoff lands it
should be shorter and plainer than the essay's own opening sentence. The reader
waits four hundred words for "an order is a pattern."

**Define in pairs when the term has a twin.** Endure and overcome.
Justification and sanctification. Obedience and sacrifice. Veil and wall. Clean
and clueless, worthy and ready. This tradition resolves difficulty by locating
both sides inside the same verse rather than defeating one — Halverson's
"proving contraries" — and it is why the tradition's definitions rarely read as
polemic.

## 3. Scaffold shapes

### Woodmansee's anomaly loop — six beats

The cleanest essay skeleton in the three corpora. It fits an 1,100-word piece
almost exactly, which is Lintel's target length.

1. A familiar thing, concretely staged.
2. Isn't that strange. — "isn't this the weirdest wedding you've ever heard
   of?"
3. The standard reading, set gently aside. — "certainly all of that is true,
   but..."
4. A withheld concrete fact — a custom, a root, an object, a political
   pressure.
5. Re-read the same text with the fact installed.
6. Compressed restatement, shorter and plainer than the buildup.

Beat 4 is a debt incurred at beat 2. If the fact is one the reader already had,
the loop has spent attention and paid nothing (tic 17).

### Halverson's objection sequence — the best complete template in the corpora

1. Voice the objection. — "Now that seems exclusivistic though, doesn't it?"
2. Concede fully, including the underlying value. — "and that's a good thing"
3. Name the overcorrection. — *exclusivistic, paternalistic, postcolonial*
   land, and then "Come on." knocks them over.
4. Return to the text as the answer.
5. Split the verse and label both halves.
6. Land on a one-word inversion.

### McLaughlin's borrowed engine

Entropy. Social contract theory. Aviation checklists. Real-estate agency.

Egypt is not *like* a covenant structure in his account; it **is** one instance
of the machine he built ten minutes earlier. This is the correct method for an
essay whose whole job is to define an abstraction, because the reader is
holding a working model before any doctrine arrives.

He is also the only one who renames institutions as mechanisms — not *the
church* or *the family* but "a covenant structure," "a spiritual domain," "an
order."

**The structural liability, which editing cannot fix.** His method depends on a
listener who cannot skip ahead. The covenant explanation runs roughly 1,400
words of physics, bears, log cabins, and Locke before it names anything. A
reader who can see the page will skim to the bolded sentence, and the
withholding collapses. In prose, shorten the runway to about a third and use no
bold — which changes what the passage does, and is the price of the medium. He
knows the diffuseness is real: "This is one of those things that's a spider
web" / "We can unwrap this forever, so I gotta be careful not to go down too
many rattles."

### The scope ladder

Extend a long sentence by climbing scope rather than by subordinating.

> "first it's your spirit body, and then it's your physical body, and it's the
> body of your marriage, your family, your ward, your community, your country"
> — **McLaughlin**

> "posterity, priesthood, and promised land" — **Halverson** (the terminal
> triple, his compressed form of the same instinct)

> "sanitizing it, flattening it, simplifying it, and removing any forms of
> worship that would indicate a higher power than the king" — **Woodmansee**

Stem anaphora works in print at reduced repetition — two beats, not four.
"This is why... This is why... This is how..."

### The default essay shape

Woodmansee's loop for the skeleton, McLaughlin's definition mechanics at the
center, Halverson's landing.

1. Open on the familiar thing made strange, in one concrete scene or one
   unexplained number. No thesis.
2. Name the standard reading and grant it a full clause.
3. Build the prerequisite — one machine, one custom, or one etymology. This is
   the longest stretch and it should feel like it is about something else.
4. Define, in one short sentence, present tense, verb *is* or *means*.
5. Cash it out, then test it on the inverse.
6. Land on worry, a question, or a procedure. Do not dwell. Stop.

### The three landings

None of the three lands an application as a command.

**Worry** — Halverson: "I do worry, my friends, if there are times where we
find ourselves wandering."

**A question turned on the reader** — Woodmansee: "Am I on that side of the
door?"

**A procedure with the literal words supplied** — McLaughlin: "what is my
actual sin? And two, teach me how to repent."

And nobody dwells. Halverson cuts immediately to the next verse number: "Please
keep that in mind. Now to help us understand how we need to be ready, Joshua
one is an incredible place to begin. Verse one..." McLaughlin's landing is
often a hinge — `Does that make sense?` followed immediately by `Okay, so
let's...`.

## 4. Transition vocabulary

This tradition does not signal logical relation. It signals **joint physical
movement through material**.

### Use

| Form | Source | Specimen |
|---|---|---|
| *Now.* | Halverson | "Now verse 12 says that Jesse had eight sons." |
| *So.* | all three | "So Bethlehem means house of bread." |
| *Well.* | all three | — |
| *Let's* at clause head | McLaughlin, ~49× | "let's block it out"; "let's pause this and step over and look at Moses"; "let's put it in our mind's eye at the top, and let's put a plumb line down" |
| Return-by-name | McLaughlin | "Let's go back to the airplane." |
| *Turn with me.* / *Keep reading.* | Halverson | — |
| Verse-number metronome | Halverson | "Verse one..."; "So look at verse 21." (Woodmansee) |
| Day/scene marker | Halverson | "So that brings us to Thursday." |
| Direct instruction that becomes a metaphor later | Halverson | "I hope you brought your hiking boots today" |

### Do not use

*However* (Halverson: 3 across the corpus, and it is his most frequent logical
connective). *Moreover* (0). *Furthermore* (1, inside a quoted letter). *Thus*,
*Consequently*, *In conclusion* (Woodmansee: 0 each). *Therefore* — an essay in
this voice that reaches for it has left the register.

### Substitution table

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| Therefore / Thus | So |
| Moreover / Furthermore | Nothing. Start the sentence. |
| However | And yet — or split into two sentences |
| In conclusion | Nothing. Land and stop. |
| It is worth noting that | Nothing. Say the thing. |
| Consider X | X is *[verb]*. Start in the middle of it. |
| Notice how X | State X. |
| As we have seen | Nothing. |

## 5. The tic blocklist, with substitutes

The numbered canonical list lives in `SKILL.md`. This is the diagnostic
version: the failure, why it happens, and what to write instead.

**Warmth ported as adjectives.** *Amazing* (Halverson, 21), *incredible*
(Halverson, 20), *really* (Woodmansee, 81). The most visible features and the
cheapest to copy, and not where the warmth lives. The warmth lives in the
self-implicating *we*, in the confession of prior failure, and in the joke
aimed at the teacher rather than at the text. **Substitute:** budget zero
intensifiers and pay for warmth structurally.

**Scholarship as name-dropping.** **Substitute:** the delete test. Remove the
technical term; if the paragraph's claim survives unchanged, the term was a
credential. Woodmansee's model is to flag her own limits rather than perform
fluency: "and I don't know if I'm going to pronounce this correctly."

**Analogy as illustration.** The imitator writes the doctrine and then adds
*it's like a...*. All three do the reverse. **Substitute:** build the mechanism,
let the doctrine fall out. Halverson's Sinai hike arrives as an instruction
before it is ever a metaphor, and the abstraction — "paradigm shift," "crowned
with commandments" — is withheld until the last sentence, when the climb has
already earned it.

**The inversion manufactured in advance.** Halverson's antimetabole is the most
tempting thing in these corpora: "I'm trying to make you different so you can
make a difference"; "not to earn anything, but to relearn"; "not called to
replace them. You're called to succeed them." It works because it arrives once,
after the argument, built on a stem the argument already established.
**Substitute:** write the argument, then check whether a word in it has already
been used twice in different senses. If not, no inversion.

**Questions without deferral.** Three questions answered in the next sentence
converts a structural device into throat-clearing. **Substitute:** Woodmansee's
gap — "I think we get a hint of our answer in the next verses." The answer is
one paragraph away, not one sentence.

**Estrangement with nothing behind it.** **Substitute:** do not raise the
anomaly unless a genuinely withheld concrete fact is in hand.

**Manufactured biography.** Woodmansee's admission works because it is true and
because she dates her turns precisely: "I will admit that at one point in my
life, I was kind of right here I had fallen praise to some of the scoffing of
the critics." Lintel essays have no author persona. **Substitute:** the
boundary-of-knowledge marker — "We don't totally know" — which requires no
biography and costs nothing in credibility.

**Application written as exhortation.** *We must.* *Let us.* Neither phrase
appears in any of these corpora. **Substitute:** worry, a question turned on
the reader, or a procedure with the exact words supplied.

**Register collision.** McLaughlin's altitude — *dominion, domain, covenant
structure* — and Halverson's — *shoes, bread, hiking boots, camels* — cannot
sit in the same paragraph. Woodmansee sits between them, Latinate only for the
names of ancient things and glossed in the plainest possible words in the same
breath: "they would sign the ketubah. It was like the wedding contract."
**Substitute:** pick one register per essay and hold it.

**Ignoring the length asymmetry.** Halverson runs five-hour episodes and
defends the cost out loud: "they'll get the same parts every four years. And
they'll skip the same parts every four years." His digressions are affordable;
McLaughlin's spider web is affordable; a 1,200-word essay affords one
digression and it has to be load-bearing. Everything in these corpora that
reads as generosity of time is a budget the essay does not have. **What
survives the compression is the sequence, not the amplitude.**

## 6. Spoken-only devices and their prose replacements

The blunt version: **every spoken emphasis device converts to positioning.** A
live teacher lands "That's not how the ancients saw it." with silence. Prose
has no silence; it has the paragraph break, and a short sentence given its own
line does the same work. That single substitution accounts for most of the
transposition.

### Transposes intact

- **The fragment landing after a long build.** All three.
- **The voiced objection.** "Now that seems exclusivistic though, doesn't it?"
  (Halverson.) This is the prose replacement for McLaughlin's live elicitation.
- **Etymology cashed out in the next sentence.** All three, and none lets it
  sit.
- **Negation-then-assertion, and definition by paired contrast.**
- **Concede-then-pivot.**
- **Scope ladders and stem anaphora**, at reduced repetition — two beats, not
  four.
- **Material specificity and arithmetic.** The Olympic shot put at 16 pounds,
  Woodmansee's 300 yards of fabric, the 125 pounds of chain mail. These are
  *better* in print than aloud, because a reader can dwell on a number.
- **The coined portable label.** "Proving contraries," "the Wednesday morning
  question," "temple glasses." Print is where these actually stick, because a
  reader can flip back.
- **The inline gloss spliced mid-quotation.** Halverson's, and the single
  highest-value transferable mechanic in this study.

### Needs repair before it transposes

**Free indirect speech.** Halverson's unmarked first person for biblical
characters — "Oh, great. It's probably him. He always shows up when you don't
want him to." — works on the page but needs punctuation and framing the
transcript does not supply. McLaughlin's ventriloquized adversary voice needs
the same repair; without it his Satan-voice and self-voice run together and
become unreadable.

**Interval repetition.** Live teachers restate because listeners drift.
Woodmansee's progressive definition — the bride defined in four installments
over several minutes — is the borderline case. It transposes only if each
installment adds a genuinely new clause and the essay is long enough to justify
the return. At 1,100 words, it usually is not.

### Spoken-only — cut, and substitute

| Device | Specimen | Substitute |
|---|---|---|
| The long verbatim read | Woodmansee reads the Isaiah 22 Eliakim block for ~200 words; Halverson reads the whole Exodus 35 offering list | Cut to the load-bearing clause. The commentary usually isolates it already: "So look at verse 21." |
| Restatement for emphasis | "So all laws have a law of sacrifice that are inextricably connected to it. So all laws have sacrifices." | Give the second version its own paragraph, delete the first |
| Elicitation from an interlocutor | "Dominion? Dominion. Okay." — McLaughlin builds the next two minutes from the word his host supplied | No prose equivalent exists. Use the voiced reader objection. |
| The comprehension hand-back | `Does that make sense?` (McLaughlin), `right?` (Woodmansee, 49×), `Okay?` / `K?` (Halverson) | Delete. Do **not** convert to a rhetorical question — that is the tempting and wrong move. |
| The quiz | "okay so quiz for you guys" — presupposes people with scriptures open | The imperative, which survives: "Study that passage in Leviticus 16 with the two goats." |
| Meta-narration of digression | "we're getting ahead of our ski tips" | If you noticed the digression, cut the digression. |
| Housekeeping and vocatives | "my friends," "folks," "we've got 55 pages of material to cover today," "I've been battling a cold all week" | Delete. |
| Trailing dependent clauses | — | Promote to their own sentence or cut. |

## 7. Verbatim model passages

Short specimens, kept for calibration. Read them before drafting; do not quote
them in an essay.

### McLaughlin — the cleanest long-build / short-landing in the corpora

> "So what makes a person powerful is that they know the law, they know what
> the terms of the law are, what it does, and they know the sacrifice
> associated with it, and they know how to put those two things together so
> that that veil is rent or parted, and they can receive from the thing that's
> on the other side of it. **We call that person a priest.**"

Eighty words of accumulation answered by six. Note that the definition is not
even the point of the sentence; it arrives as a *naming* of something the
reader has already watched work.

### Halverson — the inline gloss

> "whose height was six cubits in a span. **That's nine and a half feet
> tall.**"

He does not block-quote and then explain. He interrupts inside the quotation.
This solves the block-quote problem every doctrinal essay has.

### Halverson — arithmetic instead of assertion

> "I actually looked up the weight of an Olympic shot put, and it's amazingly
> about the same, 16 pounds."

### Halverson — scholarship handed over as a reusable rule

> "if you ever see a name that has an e l in it, that's short for Elohim"

A result would have been "this name contains a divine element." A rule is
something the reader can use on the next name they meet.

### Halverson — the boundary of knowledge, marked plainly

> "Now verse 12 says that Jesse had eight sons. In the book of first
> Chronicles, it says he had seven, so there's some discrepancy there. We don't
> totally know."

### Halverson — the self-implicating *we*

> "We guardians of the storehouse, we who live here in Bethlehem... We have to
> do better."

### McLaughlin — taking the indictment first and hardest

> "I'm saying this because I do this every day and I'm becoming more and more
> aware of it. of how much of the devil's kingdom I build every day. I build it
> every day."

### McLaughlin — the non-shaming diagnosis

His answer to failure is always "your order of operations is wrong," never "you
aren't trying hard enough." The C-130 with the throttle pushed on a cold
tarmac. Worth taking as a standing editorial policy for application paragraphs.

### Woodmansee — the deferred answer

> "Now this sets up a question for us... So was this a returning of to how
> Israel was supposed to practice their religion? Or was this a change perhaps
> for political purposes? **I think we get a hint of our answer in the next
> verses.**"

### Woodmansee — the six-word verdict after a build

> "It's the knowing that's that dividing line."

### Woodmansee — scholarship compressed into one sentence

> Margaret Barker "piece together **what Joseph Smith had delivered on a
> platter**."

No apparatus, no footnote, and the whole argument is in it. This is the model
for how to write about scholarship in these essays.

### Woodmansee — evidence you can touch

300 yards of fabric. A door with "a wooden bar that slides across." Yeast that
is "tiny, right? So it was quite a process." Palm fronds cut in Arizona. A veil
chosen "because it was pretty."

The concreteness is what makes ancient claims read as reports rather than
theories, and the domestic register is the counterweight that keeps first-
temple theology, Philo, and the Tetragrammaton from sounding academic.

## 8. Where the three are incompatible

Their hedging postures are three different theories of authority, and mixing
them makes an essay's authority illegible. Pick one per essay.

**McLaughlin hedges the frame, then speaks flatly inside it.**

> "I hate saying this is the definition of this"

— and three minutes later:

> "There's no neutral territory ever."

**Woodmansee grades each claim separately.**

> "I'm putting forward a theory that some Bible scholars would probably
> discount"

sits beside unhedged assertions, and the unhedged ones land harder because of
it.

**Halverson barely hedges definitions at all.** He hedges *inferences*:

> "Maybe he wanted both hands free... I don't know"

and states meanings flat.

An essay that hedges its frame *and* grades every claim *and* asserts its
definitions flat reads as nervous rather than careful.

The same one-per-essay rule applies to register (section 5) and to the
analogy (section 3). The three teachers are a menu, not a blend.
