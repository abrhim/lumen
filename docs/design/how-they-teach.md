# How They Teach

*A comparative study of Jared Halverson, Todd McLaughlin, and Andrea Woodmansee, for a writer who has to produce doctrinal essays in a voice drawn from all three.*

---

## 0. The thesis, stated up front so you can disagree with it

The obvious reading of these three is that they share warmth: friendly address, self-deprecation, a pastoral register. That reading will produce bad imitations, because warmth is the *output* of their method, not the method.

What they actually share is **withholding**. All three are, mechanically, teachers of deferred payoff. Halverson front-loads the frame and back-loads the point. McLaughlin builds a working non-scriptural machine for two hundred words before naming what it is: "Okay, so this is the idea of covenant." Woodmansee opens with what you already assume, breaks it with a stacked pair of questions, and does not answer for a paragraph: "I think we get a hint of our answer in the next verses."

Every one of their signature devices — the fragment landing, the voiced objection, the etymology, the extended analogy — is a mechanism for making the reader wait, and then for making the arrival smaller and plainer than the wait. Get the withholding right and the warmth follows. Port the warmth without the withholding and you get an essay that sounds enthusiastic about nothing in particular.

## 1. The common grammar

### The sentence economy is identical across all three

Halverson: mean 14.4 words, median 12. McLaughlin: mean 13.4, median 10, 25% of sentences ≤5 words. Woodmansee: mean 14.1, median 10, 25.7% ≤5 words. Three independent corpora, three effectively identical distributions. This is not coincidence; it is what the shared move requires.

The unit is **long accumulating build, short landing**. McLaughlin's is the cleanest specimen — an eighty-word chained sentence answered by six words:

> "So what makes a person powerful is that they know the law, they know what the terms of the law are, what it does, and they know the sacrifice associated with it, and they know how to put those two things together so that that veil is rent or parted, and they can receive from the thing that's on the other side of it. **We call that person a priest.**"

Halverson does the same thing with an inversion instead of a definition: "I'm trying to make you different so you can make a difference." Woodmansee does it with a verdict: "It's the knowing that's that dividing line."

The rule for the essay: never two long sentences in a row. After any sentence over thirty words, the next sentence is a question or a verdict, and it is under twelve words.

### Long sentences are built by accumulation, never by subordination

None of the three writes a periodic sentence. They chain with *and*, *so*, *because*, *which means*, and they extend by listing concrete items or by climbing a ladder of scope. McLaughlin's scope ladder is the most systematic — "first it's your spirit body, and then it's your physical body, and it's the body of your marriage, your family, your ward, your community, your country" — but Halverson has the same instinct in his terminal triples ("posterity, priesthood, and promised land"), and Woodmansee in her serial lists ("sanitizing it, flattening it, simplifying it, and removing any forms of worship that would indicate a higher power than the king").

### Transitions are deictic or imperative, not logical

Count the absences. Halverson: *however* 3, *moreover* 0, *furthermore* 1 (inside a quoted letter). Woodmansee: no *Moreover*, no *Furthermore*, no *Thus*, no *Consequently*, no *In conclusion*. McLaughlin transitions almost exclusively with a joint imperative — `let's` roughly 49 times at clause head: "let's block it out," "let's pause this and step over and look at Moses," "let's put it in our mind's eye at the top, and let's put a plumb line down."

This tradition does not signal logical relation. It signals **joint physical movement through material**. *Now.* *So.* *Well.* *Let's.* *Turn with me.* *Keep reading.* *So that brings us to Thursday.* An essay in this voice that reaches for *therefore* has left the register.

### The received reading is graded, never discarded

This is the most important shared move and the one that keeps all three from reading as contrarian, which matters enormously for doctrinal prose. The formula is literally *X is true, and there's more* — never *X is wrong*.

McLaughlin: "Two-way promise, right? Which isn't a wrong answer, but it's not a sufficient, it's not a comprehensive answer."
Woodmansee: "certainly all of that is true, but I think there's a big piece that we're missing."
Halverson: "Now we talk about being a peculiar people, and we think of peculiar as in weird. And yes, sometimes we live up to that."

Note that in every case the standard reading gets a *full clause of respect* before the pivot. Halverson goes further and concedes the underlying value of the objection outright — on inclusivity, "and that's a good thing" — before he criticizes the overcorrection. The concession is not tactical politeness; it is what buys the reading that follows.

### The "we" is self-implicating

None of them uses the safe pastoral *we* that means *you*. Halverson: "We guardians of the storehouse, we who live here in Bethlehem... We have to do better." McLaughlin takes the indictment first and hardest: "I'm saying this because I do this every day and I'm becoming more and more aware of it. of how much of the devil's kingdom I build every day. I build it every day." Woodmansee: "I never stopped to consider... Why am I doing this?"

### One analogy per argument, and it carries the argument

All three flag their figures as instruments and then exhaust them. Halverson's Sinai hike maps darkness, dawn, summit, camels, and carved stairs, each cashed for a doctrinal claim before he opens the text. McLaughlin returns to his by name — "Let's go back to the airplane." Woodmansee's TikTok solar system is a fully worked mundane mechanism followed by exactly one mapping sentence.

The test they all pass and imitators fail: **delete the analogy and see whether the claim survives.** If it survives, the analogy was decorative.

### The landing is short, plain, and slightly deflationary, and nobody dwells

Halverson cuts immediately to the next verse number: "Please keep that in mind. Now to help us understand how we need to be ready, Joshua one is an incredible place to begin. Verse one..." McLaughlin's landing is often a hinge — `Does that make sense?` followed immediately by `Okay, so let's...`. Woodmansee's payoff sentence "is usually shorter and plainer than the buildup."

And none of the three lands an application as a command. They land it as **worry** (Halverson: "I do worry, my friends, if there are times where we find ourselves wandering"), as a **question turned on the reader** (Woodmansee: "Am I on that side of the door?"), or as a **procedure with the literal words supplied** (McLaughlin: "what is my actual sin? And two, teach me how to repent"). Nobody writes *we must*. Nobody writes *let us*.

### The boundary of knowledge is marked out loud

Halverson: "Now verse 12 says that Jesse had eight sons. In the book of first Chronicles, it says he had seven, so there's some discrepancy there. We don't totally know." Woodmansee: "whether that's good or bad is the scholars debate." McLaughlin: "When I suggest this, I could be just dead wrong, and I'm very open to that."

This is a genre requirement, not a personality trait. In doctrinal prose the unhedged claims only land hard if the hedged ones were hedged honestly.

## 2. Where they differ, and what to take from whom

| | Engine | Best for | Signature you can steal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Halverson** | The text is the spine; the verse number is the metronome | Narrative passages, closings, handling an objection generously | The aphoristic inversion; the inline gloss spliced mid-verse |
| **McLaughlin** | Build a working non-scriptural machine, then run scripture through it | Abstract system terms — covenant, priesthood, altar, repentance | The prerequisite chain; the order-of-operations diagnosis |
| **Woodmansee** | Estrangement — make a familiar text stop making sense | Openings; ritual objects; anything the reader thinks they know | The anomaly loop; material and tactile evidence |

### Halverson: the only one whose method never leaves the page

His subjects are people, not abstractions. The Latinate words appear at exactly two moments, and one of them is as a target: *exclusivistic, paternalistic, postcolonial* land, and then "Come on." knocks them over. His scholarship is handed over as a reusable rule rather than a result — "if you ever see a name that has an e l in it, that's short for Elohim" — and his quantification is arithmetic rather than assertion: "I actually looked up the weight of an Olympic shot put, and it's amazingly about the same, 16 pounds."

**Borrow him for:** any passage that walks a scriptural narrative; any closing sentence; any place where you have to grant an objection real weight before answering it. His argumentative sequence — voice the objection, concede fully, name the overcorrection, return to the text as the answer, split the verse and label both halves, land on a one-word inversion — is the best complete template in the three corpora.

**Borrow his gloss technique everywhere.** He does not block-quote and then explain; he interrupts inside the quotation: "whose height was six cubits in a span. **That's nine and a half feet tall.**" This is the single highest-value transferable mechanic in this study, because it solves the block-quote problem that all doctrinal essays have.

### McLaughlin: the most systematic and the least directly portable

He is the only one who *renames institutions as mechanisms*. He does not say the church or the family; he says "a covenant structure," "a spiritual domain," "an order." His definitions are the tightest of the three because he pays a long setup and takes compression as the reward: "an order is a pattern." "sin is resistance to the spirit or the light of God in any domain."

His two unique gifts:

**The borrowed engine.** Entropy, social contract theory, aviation checklists, real-estate agency. Egypt is not *like* a covenant structure in his account; it *is* one instance of the machine he built ten minutes earlier. This is the correct method for an essay whose whole job is to define an abstraction, because it means the reader is holding a working model before any doctrine arrives.

**The non-shaming diagnosis.** His answer to failure is always "your order of operations is wrong," never "you aren't trying hard enough." The C-130 with the throttle pushed on a cold tarmac. This is worth taking as a standing editorial policy for application paragraphs.

**But note the liability, which is structural and not fixable by editing.** His method depends on a listener who cannot skip ahead. The covenant explanation runs roughly 1,400 words of physics, bears, log cabins, and Locke before it names anything. A reader who can see the page will skim to the bolded sentence, and the withholding collapses. In prose you must shorten his runway to perhaps a third, which changes what the passage does. He knows the diffuseness is real: "This is one of those things that's a spider web" / "We can unwrap this forever, so I gotta be careful not to go down too many rattles."

### Woodmansee: the most portable single move, and the most dangerous

Her engine is estrangement, not information. "isn't this the weirdest wedding you've ever heard of?" The historical fact is only ever the *release* of a tension she built. Her six-beat loop — familiar thing, isn't that strange, the standard reading set gently aside, a withheld concrete fact, re-read the same text with the fact installed, compressed restatement — is the cleanest essay skeleton available in these three corpora, and it fits an 1,100-word piece almost exactly.

Her second gift is **evidence you can touch**: 300 yards of fabric, doors with a wooden bar that slides across, yeast that is "tiny, right? So it was quite a process." The concreteness is what makes ancient claims read as reports rather than theories. Her domestic register is the counterweight that keeps first-temple theology, Philo, and the Tetragrammaton from sounding academic — palm fronds cut in Arizona, a veil chosen "because it was pretty."

Her best compression is also her best model for how to write about scholarship: Margaret Barker "piece together **what Joseph Smith had delivered on a platter**." One sentence, no apparatus, and the whole argument is in it.

**Borrow her for openings, always.** All three open well, but hers is the only opening that works on a page a reader can abandon.

### The one place they are genuinely incompatible

Their hedging postures are three different theories of authority, and mixing them makes an essay's authority illegible.

- **McLaughlin hedges the frame, then speaks flatly inside it.** "I hate saying this is the definition of this" — and three minutes later, "There's no neutral territory ever."
- **Woodmansee grades each claim separately.** "I'm putting forward a theory that some Bible scholars would probably discount" sits beside unhedged assertions, and the unhedged ones land harder because of it.
- **Halverson barely hedges definitions at all.** He hedges *inferences* — "Maybe he wanted both hands free... I don't know" — and states meanings flat.

Pick one per essay. An essay that hedges its frame *and* grades every claim *and* asserts its definitions flat reads as nervous rather than careful.

## 3. Imitable in prose vs. spoken-only

The blunt version: **every spoken emphasis device converts to positioning.** A live teacher lands "That's not how the ancients saw it." with silence. Prose has no silence; it has the paragraph break, and a short sentence given its own line does the same work. That single substitution accounts for most of the transposition.

### Imitable, more or less directly

- **The fragment landing after a long build.** All three, and it survives intact.
- **The voiced objection.** "Now that seems exclusivistic though, doesn't it?" This is the prose replacement for McLaughlin's live elicitation and should be used wherever he would have asked the host a question.
- **Free indirect speech.** Halverson's unmarked first person for biblical characters — "Oh, great. It's probably him. He always shows up when you don't want him to." Works on the page, but needs punctuation and framing the transcript does not supply. McLaughlin's ventriloquized adversary voice needs the same repair; without it his Satan-voice and self-voice run together and become unreadable.
- **Etymology cashed out in the next sentence.** All three do it and none lets it sit. "Endowment means gift." is three words and is the whole definition.
- **Negation-then-assertion, and definition by paired contrast.** Fully portable.
- **Concede-then-pivot.** Fully portable.
- **Scope ladders and stem anaphora.** "This is why... This is why... This is how..." Works in print at reduced repetition — two beats, not four.
- **Material specificity and arithmetic.** The shot put, the 300 yards, the 125 pounds of chain mail. These are *better* in print than aloud, because a reader can dwell on a number.
- **The coined portable label.** "Proving contraries," "the Wednesday morning question," "temple glasses." Print is where these actually stick, because a reader can flip back.
- **The inline gloss spliced mid-quotation.** Halverson's, and it is the fix for the block-quote problem below.

### Spoken-only, and what to substitute

- **The long verbatim read.** Woodmansee reads the Isaiah 22 Eliakim block for roughly 200 words; Halverson reads the entire Exodus 35 offering list. Aloud, length *is* the argument — the audience feels the density before it gets sorted. On a page, readers skip block quotes. Cut to the load-bearing clause. Her own commentary already isolates it for you: "So look at verse 21."
- **Restatement for emphasis.** "So all laws have a law of sacrifice that are inextricably connected to it. So all laws have sacrifices." Aloud that is emphasis; in print it is redundancy. Substitute: give the second version its own paragraph and delete the first.
- **Elicitation from an interlocutor.** "Dominion? Dominion. Okay." McLaughlin builds the next two minutes out of the word his host supplies, and there is no prose equivalent at all. Substitute the voiced reader objection.
- **The comprehension hand-back.** `Does that make sense?` (McLaughlin, near every major beat), `right?` (Woodmansee, 49 times), `Okay?` and `K?` (Halverson). These invite a listener to say no. A reader cannot. Delete all of them; do not convert them into rhetorical questions, which is the tempting and wrong move.
- **The quiz.** "okay so quiz for you guys" presupposes people with scriptures open. Substitute the imperative — "Study that passage in Leviticus 16 with the two goats" — which survives.
- **Meta-narration of digression.** "we're getting ahead of our ski tips." On the page, if you noticed the digression, cut it.
- **Housekeeping and vocatives.** "my friends," "folks," "we've got 55 pages of material to cover today," "I've been battling a cold all week."
- **Interval repetition.** Live teachers restate because listeners drift. Woodmansee's progressive definition — the bride defined in four installments over several minutes — is the borderline case. It transposes only if each installment adds a genuinely new clause and the essay is long enough to justify the return.
- **Trailing dependent clauses.** Fine aloud, broken in print.

## 4. The definition pattern

This is the load-bearing section, because a doctrinal essay's first job is to define a term, and all three converge on a five-move shape.

**Move 1 — State the received meaning in its strongest form and grant it.**
Not a strawman. The version a thoughtful reader actually holds, given a full clause of respect. "Two-way promise, right? Which isn't a wrong answer, but it's not a sufficient, it's not a comprehensive answer." / "Certainly that happens in some cases. But I don't believe that was the original meaning."

**Move 2 — Withhold the term while you build what it needs.**
Three routes, pick by term type.
*Prerequisite chain* (McLaughlin, for system terms): dominion, then the unseen world, then agency, then veils, and only then altar. *Withheld cultural fact* (Woodmansee, for ritual and narrative terms): the token at the door, the wooden bolt, the recognition of the voice. *Etymology decomposed* (Halverson, for names and loaded words): "Beth, beit means house and lechem means bread."

**Move 3 — The definition itself: one short present-tense sentence, verb *is* or *means*.**
Never "X is defined as." Halverson uses *means* 25 times: "X doesn't mean A. It means B." McLaughlin uses deflationary *just*: "righteousness just means that you are rightly aligned to a domain of God." Woodmansee uses contrast: "veiling was considered to be a mark of sanctity" — eight words, after the ground is cleared.

If the term is abstract, choose a **deliberately generic genus and load the differentia** (McLaughlin's "a structure," "a practice," "a system or a platform"). If the term is a role or an ordinance, **define by function**: "an altar is how you traverse a veil. It's how you part one."

**Move 4 — Cash it out in the same breath.**
No definition is allowed to stand as information. "So Bethlehem means house of bread. What better place for the the bread of life himself to be born?" "The Hebrew words for eternity and conceal actually share the same root... So eternity, in a sense, is something that is concealed."

**Move 5 — Test it on the inverse or the edge.**
McLaughlin runs the altar definition against Cain immediately: "Satan or the adversary or the dark domains do not have altars that are independent of themselves." Woodmansee corrects her own inversion before it overshoots: "But the interesting thing is, it's not a wall right it's a veil and so a veil is meant to be traversed." Halverson pairs the term with its twin in a column — justification and sanctification, works and grace, replace and succeed.

**Three governing constraints.**

*The hedge budget is one.* McLaughlin's "I hate saying this is the definition of this, right? This is maybe a really good way of thinking about it" is deliberate and doctrinally motivated — he offers definitions as instruments rather than verdicts. But Woodmansee's stack — "So it's possible that... I find it to be very likely that... I think... I believe..." — is three hedges in four sentences, and in prose that reads as evasion.

*The definition is the reward, not the setup.* When the payoff lands it should be shorter and plainer than the essay's own opening sentence. This is the mechanical form of the withholding thesis: the reader waits four hundred words for "an order is a pattern."

*Define in pairs when the term has a twin.* Endure and overcome. Justification and sanctification. Obedience and sacrifice. Veil and wall. Clean and clueless, worthy and ready. This tradition resolves difficulty by locating both sides inside the same verse rather than defeating one, which is what Halverson means by "proving contraries" — and it is why the tradition's definitions rarely read as polemic.

## 5. What a first attempt will get wrong

**Warmth ported as adjectives.** Halverson's corpus has *amazing* 21 times and *incredible* 20; Woodmansee's has *really* 81. These are the most visible features and the cheapest to copy, and they are not where the warmth lives. The warmth lives in the self-implicating *we*, in the confession of prior failure, in the joke aimed at the teacher rather than the text. An imitator takes the adjectives, drops the structure, and produces enthusiasm without cause. Budget: zero intensifiers. Warmth is a structural expense.

**Scholarship as name-dropping.** The corpus rule is harsher than most writers expect. McLaughlin uses *exogenous* exactly once and *apophatic* never returns. Halverson uses Hebrew twice in ten episodes, both times because the English is misleading. Woodmansee has zero instances of *typology*, *exegesis*, *hermeneutic*, *chiasm*, or *epistem-* across 26,800 words, and flags her own uncertainty rather than performing fluency: "and I don't know if I'm going to pronounce this correctly." The test: delete the technical term. If the paragraph's claim survives unchanged, the term was a credential.

**Analogy as illustration.** The imitator writes the doctrine and then adds *it's like a...*. All three do the reverse: build the mechanism, let the doctrine fall out. Halverson's Sinai hike arrives as an instruction ("I hope you brought your hiking boots today") before it is ever a metaphor, and the abstraction — "paradigm shift," "crowned with commandments" — is withheld until the last sentence, when the climb has already earned it.

**The inversion manufactured in advance.** Halverson's antimetabole is the most tempting thing in these corpora — "make you different so you can make a difference," "not to earn anything, but to relearn," "not called to replace them. You're called to succeed them." It works because it arrives once, after the argument, built on a stem the argument already established. If you can write the inversion before you write the argument, the inversion is fake, and it will read as a fortune cookie. One per essay, maximum, and only if the changed word was earned earlier.

**Questions without deferral.** All three stack questions; all three withhold. An imitator asks three questions and answers them in the next sentence, which converts a structural device into throat-clearing. Woodmansee's discipline is the model: "Now this sets up a question for us... So was this a returning of to how Israel was supposed to practice their religion? Or was this a change perhaps for political purposes? I think we get a hint of our answer in the next verses." The answer is one paragraph away, not one sentence.

**Estrangement with nothing behind it.** "isn't this the weirdest wedding you've ever heard of?" is a debt. If you make a text strange and then discharge the tension with a fact the reader already had, you have spent attention and paid nothing. Every anomaly needs a genuinely withheld concrete fact — a custom, a root, an object, a political pressure — or it should not be raised.

**Manufactured biography.** "I will admit that at one point in my life, I was kind of right here I had fallen praise to some of the scoffing of the critics" works because it is true and because she dates her turns precisely. If the writer has no such history with the material, the substitute is the boundary-of-knowledge marker — "We don't totally know" — which requires no biography and costs nothing in credibility.

**Application written as exhortation.** *We must.* *Let us.* Neither phrase appears in any of these corpora. The tradition's applications arrive as worry, as a question turned on the reader, or as a procedure with the exact words supplied.

**Register collision.** McLaughlin's abstraction level — *dominion, domain, covenant structure* — and Halverson's — *shoes, bread, hiking boots, camels* — cannot sit in the same paragraph. Woodmansee sits between them: Latinate only for the names of ancient things, glossed in the plainest possible words in the same breath ("they would sign the ketubah. It was like the wedding contract"). Pick one register per essay and hold it. A paragraph that visibly mixes all three reads as a composite, which is exactly the thing to avoid.

**Ignoring the length asymmetry.** Halverson runs five-hour episodes and defends the cost out loud: "they'll get the same parts every four years. And they'll skip the same parts every four years." His digressions are affordable; McLaughlin's spider web is affordable; a 1,200-word essay affords one digression, and it has to be load-bearing. Everything in these corpora that reads as generosity of time is a budget the essay does not have. What survives the compression is the *sequence*, not the amplitude.

## 6. A default shape, offered so you can reject it

Woodmansee's anomaly loop for the skeleton, McLaughlin's definition mechanics at the center, Halverson's landing:

1. Open on the familiar thing made strange, in one concrete scene or one unexplained number. No thesis.
2. Name the standard reading and grant it a full clause.
3. Build the prerequisite — one machine, one custom, or one etymology. This is the longest stretch and it should feel like it is about something else.
4. Define, in one short sentence, present tense, verb *is* or *means*.
5. Cash it out, then test it on the inverse.
6. Land on worry, a question, or a procedure. Do not dwell. Stop.

The part that is hardest to keep and easiest to lose is step 3, because it looks like padding to an editor and like digression to a first-draft writer. It is neither. It is the entire mechanism by which step 4 lands as a reward rather than as information.
